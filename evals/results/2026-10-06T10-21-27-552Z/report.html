**What 4.3 says.** It makes two claims:

1. *"Odometry error is unbounded: each step adds error that is never removed, so position error grows with distance travelled."*
2. *"Heading error is the worst kind because a heading error of e radians becomes a position error of about d * e after a further distance d."*

**Claim 1.** The robot builds its pose by adding to it every step (4.2: `x += …`, `y += …`, `theta += …`). Nothing in those updates checks the pose against the outside world. So any small mistake in one step gets carried into every later pose, and the next step adds its own mistake on top. Errors pile up and nothing removes them. That's what "unbounded" means here.

**Claim 2: why heading is worst.** Look again at the pose update in 4.2:

`x += dS * cos(theta + dtheta/2)`, `y += dS * sin(theta + dtheta/2)`

`theta` sets the direction in which every future `dS` is added. Suppose `theta` is wrong by `e` radians:

- Each later step moves the robot `dS` in a direction that is off by `e`.
- So each step puts the estimate a sideways distance of about `dS * e` from where it should be.
- After a further distance `d` (the sum of all those `dS`), the sideways errors add up to about `d * e`.

The key word is "further". One heading mistake keeps causing new position error on every step after it, for as long as the robot drives. My reading of why the notes call it "the worst kind" is the contrast with a distance mistake. If one step's `dS` is wrong, the position is off by that amount once, and later steps don't make it bigger. A heading mistake works like a multiplier on all the distance still to come.

**From outside the material:**
- **Where "about" comes from.** The exact sideways error is `d * sin(e)`. For small angles `sin(e) ≈ e`, which gives the notes' `d * e`. The notes don't say this.
- **A worked example (numbers are mine).** Take e = 0.01 rad, which is about 0.57°. After a further 10 m the error is about 0.01 × 10 = 0.1 m. After 100 m it is about 1 m. The angle error stays tiny, but the position error keeps growing with distance.
- **An analogy.** You set off walking with your compass misread by one degree. At the start you can't notice it. After a kilometre you are about 17 m off course. The analogy has one fixed heading error. In odometry, new heading errors usually keep being added every step, which is worse than the analogy shows.

Section 4.3 says two things. The first:

> "Odometry error is unbounded: each step adds error that is never removed, so position error grows with distance travelled."

Odometry only ever adds to the pose. Look at the update in 4.2: `x += …`, `y += …`, `theta += …`. Each step starts from the previous estimate, and nothing in those equations checks that estimate against the real world. So any error in one step's `dS` or `dtheta` carries into every later step and stays there. More steps (more distance) means more errors piled up, and there's no ceiling. That's what "unbounded" means here.

The second:

> "Heading error is the worst kind because a heading error of e radians becomes a position error of about d * e after a further distance d."

Here's how that follows from the 4.2 update, one step at a time:

1. Suppose the estimated `theta` is wrong by `e` radians. Because of `theta += dtheta`, that error is never removed, so it's still there in every later step (that's 4.3's first sentence).
2. Each later step moves the estimate by `dS * cos(theta + dtheta/2)` and `dS * sin(theta + dtheta/2)`. That's the right distance `dS`, but in a direction that is off by `e`.
3. So each step lands a little to the side of where the robot really went. The sideways miss per step is about `dS * e`.
4. Add those misses up over a further distance `d` (the sum of the `dS`) and the total position error is about `d * e`.

My reading of why the notes call this "the worst kind": compare it with an error in distance. If one step's `dS` is wrong by some amount, the position is off by that amount once, and it doesn't grow. A heading error of `e` makes new position error on every step after it. The further the robot drives, the larger that error gets. The notes don't spell out this comparison; it's my inference from the 4.2 equations.

**From outside the material:**
- **Why "about":** step 3 uses a small-angle approximation. The exact sideways miss is `dS * sin(e)`, and for small `e`, `sin(e) ≈ e`.
- **A number to make it concrete:** a heading error of 0.01 rad (about half a degree) followed by 10 m of driving gives about 0.1 m of position error. Drive 100 m and it's about 1 m. A one-off 1 cm distance error would still be 1 cm.
- **An analogy:** you start a long walk with your compass reading half a degree off. Every pace is the right length, but you end up further and further from where you meant to go.
- **What the analogy doesn't capture:** the walker's compass error is fixed. A real robot usually keeps adding heading error every step, so it grows over time, and position error then grows faster than `d * e`. Section 4.3 only covers a single fixed `e`.

To check it landed: using `dtheta = (dS_R - dS_L) / L` from 4.2, explain in one sentence why unequal wheel radii (one of the sources in 4.4) cause heading error even when the robot is trying to drive straight.

**What 4.3 says.** Your notes make two claims:

1. *"Odometry error is unbounded: each step adds error that is never removed, so position error grows with distance travelled."*
2. *"Heading error is the worst kind because a heading error of e radians becomes a position error of about d * e after a further distance d."*

**Claim 1 in plain words.** Odometry only ever adds one step onto the last. In 4.2 every update is `x += …`, `y += …`, `theta += …`. Nothing in those equations checks the pose against the outside world. So if a step adds a small mistake, that mistake stays in x, y or theta for good, and the next step adds its own on top. The longer you drive, the more steps there are, so the error has no ceiling.

**Claim 2: why heading is different.** Look again at the position update in 4.2:

- `x += dS * cos(theta + dtheta/2)`
- `y += dS * sin(theta + dtheta/2)`

theta sets the direction of every later step. Say theta is wrong by e at some moment. From then on, every step of length dS points e radians away from where the robot is really going. Each step lands about dS · e off to the side. Over a further distance d those side errors add up to about d · e. That's the "about d * e" in your notes. The "about" is there because it's an approximation that works when e is small.

**Why that makes it "the worst".** My reading, which the notes don't spell out: compare a mistake in distance. If one step's dS is a bit wrong, the position is off by that amount once, and later steps don't make it bigger. A heading mistake is different because it's multiplied by every metre you drive afterwards. A tiny angle turns into a large position error just by continuing to drive. That's why 4.3 picks out heading.

**Link to 4.4.** Two of the systematic sources there, unequal wheel radii and an inaccurate wheelbase L, feed into `dtheta = (dS_R - dS_L) / L`. So they create heading error, the kind 4.3 calls worst. My reading is that this is why 4.5 spends a whole method (UMBmark) separating those two effects.

**From outside the material:** a number to make it concrete. Take e = 0.01 rad, which is about half a degree and hard to notice. After d = 10 m the robot is about 0.1 m off course. After 100 m it's about 1 m off. An analogy: a golf putt. Being half a degree off on the angle hardly matters for a short putt but misses the hole on a long one, and getting the strength slightly wrong doesn't get worse with distance in the same way. Where the analogy breaks: a putt has one angle error, but a robot picks up a new small heading error on every step. So in reality the position error can grow faster than d · e. Your notes give only the single-error case.

Lecture 4: Wheel odometry (notes)

4.1 Differential drive. A differential-drive robot has two driven wheels of radius r on an axle of length L (the wheelbase). If the left and right wheels turn by angles dphi_L and dphi_R in a time step, the wheels travel dS_L = r * dphi_L and dS_R = r * dphi_R along the ground.

4.2 Pose update. The distance travelled by the robot's centre is dS = (dS_R + dS_L) / 2 and the change of heading is dtheta = (dS_R - dS_L) / L. With heading theta, the pose update is x += dS * cos(theta + dtheta/2), y += dS * sin(theta + dtheta/2), theta += dtheta. The half-angle term is used because the robot turns during the step, so the mean heading over the step is a better estimate than the heading at its start.

4.3 Error growth. Odometry error is unbounded: each step adds error that is never removed, so position error grows with distance travelled. Heading error is the worst kind because a heading error of e radians becomes a position error of about d * e after a further distance d.

4.4 Sources of error. The notes list three systematic sources: unequal wheel radii, an inaccurate wheelbase L, and finite encoder resolution. Wheel slip is a non-systematic source; it cannot be calibrated out.

4.5 Calibration. The UMBmark method drives the robot around a square path clockwise and anticlockwise and uses the two end-position errors to separate the effect of unequal wheel radii from the effect of a wrong wheelbase.
